# Patient-facing side: short validated questionnaires (PROMs) and UX rules for a post-discharge self-reporting web app (stroke, carpal tunnel release, CIPN)

Research date: 21 Nov 2026. Access note: PubMed Central (pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov), PubMed and Europe PMC were blocked by bot checks in this session, so several open-access papers could only be identified by title, not read. Every bullet under "Cited Findings" was read on the linked page in this session unless it is marked otherwise. Items I could not verify are listed under "Gaps" and labelled "unverified recall" where they come from background knowledge rather than a page read today.

Licence rule of thumb used throughout: a hackathon demo may ask its own plainly worded questions about the same symptoms, but must not copy item wording or label a screen "BCTQ", "QuickDASH", "QLQ-CIPN20", "FACT/GOG-NTX", "SIS" or "PROMIS" unless the licence conditions are met.

## 1. Which short validated instruments fit each population (items, scoring, licence, MCID, Slovenian versions; daily vs weekly)

### Takeaway
No validated instrument found is designed for daily use: recall periods are 7 days (FACT/GOG-NTX, PROMIS-type), 2 weeks (BCTQ, PHQ-2), so they belong in a weekly or pre-visit slot, and the daily check-in has to be built from single self-authored items on the same constructs. Licences are the main constraint: QuickDASH forbids any modification, FACIT and EORTC require registration, SIS goes through a permission request/MAPI, while PHQ-2 and PROMIS-10 are the least restricted; the only Slovenian version confirmed today is QuickDASH (translated 2014, no published Slovenian psychometrics).

### Cited Findings

**Stroke**
- ICHOM stroke standard set uses PROMIS-10 (10 items: general health, quality of life, physical health, mental health, social satisfaction/roles, physical activities, emotional problems, fatigue, pain), plus single items on ambulation, toileting, dressing, feeding and communication, plus the simplified modified Rankin Scale questionnaire (smRSq). Timepoints: discharge + 7 days and 90 days after admission. — [ICHOM Stroke Reference Guide](https://ichom.org/files/medical-conditions/stroke/stroke-reference-guide.pdf)
- ICHOM states PROMIS-10 is free for healthcare organisations with no licence needed; scoring = two sums (Global Physical Health from 4 items, Global Mental Health from 4 items, three items re-coded) converted to T-scores (mean 50, SD 10, US general population), valid only if all items are answered. Note: the guide refers to v1.1 and lists only a few translations, so it is dated. — [ICHOM Stroke Reference Guide](https://ichom.org/files/medical-conditions/stroke/stroke-reference-guide.pdf)
- smRSq: no fee or permission needed to ask the questions in clinical practice; reusing the published flow-chart figure costs USD 700 (publisher rights); takes under 2 minutes; ICHOM classes it as clinician-reported and tells users to draw on caregivers and, when between two grades, record the worse one. — [ICHOM Stroke Reference Guide](https://ichom.org/files/medical-conditions/stroke/stroke-reference-guide.pdf)
- PROMIS Global Health takes 2–5 minutes and is listed as free. In a stroke sample (n=1,102, mean age 60.8) test–retest ICCs were 0.86 (mental) and 0.88 (physical); the global items discriminated stroke severity less well than PROMIS domain scales. No MCID is given on the page. — [Shirley Ryan AbilityLab: PROMIS Global Health](https://www.sralab.org/rehabilitation-measures/promis-global-health)
- PROMIS: more than 400 measures in more than 80 languages; the language-by-measure list is a downloadable file that could not be opened here. — [HealthMeasures: Available translations](https://www.healthmeasures.net/explore-measurement-systems/promis/intro-to-promis/available-translations)
- Stroke Impact Scale 3.0: 59 items, 8 domains (strength 4, hand function 5, ADL/IADL 10, mobility 9, communication 7, emotion 9, memory/thinking 7, participation 8); each domain scored 0–100; 15–20 minutes; free for non-profit users, others directed to MAPI Research Trust; proxy version exists and proxies tend to rate patients as more impaired. — [Shirley Ryan AbilityLab: SIS](https://www.sralab.org/rehabilitation-measures/stroke-impact-scale)
- SIS change thresholds in chronic stroke: hand function MCID 17.8 (MDC 25.9), strength MCID 9.2 (MDC 24.0), ADL/IADL MCID 5.9 (MDC 17.3), mobility MCID 4.5 (MDC 15.1). The MDC exceeds the MCID in every domain, so small week-to-week changes are within measurement noise. — [Shirley Ryan AbilityLab: SIS](https://www.sralab.org/rehabilitation-measures/stroke-impact-scale)
- SIS-16: 16 items from the four physical domains (strength, hand function, mobility, ADL/IADL); 5–10 minutes; free but permission must be requested; MCID 9.4–14.1 in subacute stroke (n=36); SRD 13.2; test–retest ICC 0.95; can be completed by proxy; more sensitive than the Barthel Index after mild stroke. — [Shirley Ryan AbilityLab: SIS-16](https://www.sralab.org/rehabilitation-measures/stroke-impact-scale-16)
- PHQ-2: two items (loss of interest/pleasure; feeling down/hopeless), past 2 weeks, each 0–3, total 0–6; cut-off 3 or more; at that cut-off sensitivity 82.9% and specificity 90.0% for major depression in a 580-patient primary-care validation; a positive screen should be followed by PHQ-9. (General-population validation, not stroke-specific.) — [National HIV Curriculum (Univ. of Washington): PHQ-2](https://www.hiv.uw.edu/page/mental-health-screening/phq-2)
- Fatigue and pain are already single items inside PROMIS-10, so one fatigue item is consistent with the ICHOM set. — [ICHOM Stroke Reference Guide](https://ichom.org/files/medical-conditions/stroke/stroke-reference-guide.pdf)

**Carpal tunnel release (CTR)**
- Boston/Brigham Carpal Tunnel Questionnaire (BCTQ): 19 items = Symptom Severity Scale (SSS) 11 items + Functional Status Scale (FSS) 8 items; each item 1–5; each scale = mean of its items (range 1–5, higher = worse); recall = a typical day in the past 2 weeks. — [Physiopedia: BCTQ](https://www.physio-pedia.com/Boston_Carpal_Tunnel_Syndrome_Questionnaire_(BCTQ)); [Leite et al. 2006, BMC Musculoskelet Disord, doi:10.1186/1471-2474-7-78](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/1471-2474-7-78)
- BCTQ completion time: mean 5.6 minutes (SD 3.5) in one study; under 10 minutes overall. Test–retest r = 0.91 (SSS) and 0.93 (FSS) in the original study. Responsiveness after surgery is large: SSS effect size 1.13 at 13.5 weeks to 2.33 at 27 weeks; FSS 0.48 at 6 weeks to 1.44 at 27 weeks (function lags symptoms). — [Leite et al. 2006](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/1471-2474-7-78)
- BCTQ MCID values conflict by method and setting: total 0.74; about 0.8 (SSS) and 0.5 (FSS) from a satisfaction anchor — [Leite et al. 2006](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/1471-2474-7-78); SSS 1.04 in 28 injection patients, FSS not determined — [Physiopedia: BCTQ](https://www.physio-pedia.com/Boston_Carpal_Tunnel_Syndrome_Questionnaire_(BCTQ)); 0.64 (SSS) and 0.23 (FSS) one month after surgery or manual therapy in 120 women — [López-de-Uralde-Villanueva et al. 2023, Arch Phys Med Rehabil, PMID 37582474 (abstract via QxMD)](https://read.qxmd.com/read/37582474/minimal-clinically-important-differences-in-hand-pain-intensity-numerical-pain-rate-scale-and-related-function-boston-carpal-tunnel-questionnaire-in-women-with-carpal-tunnel-syndrome)
- Single-item hand pain (0–10 NPRS) MCID in the same CTS cohort: 1.5 points for mean pain, 2.5 points for worst pain, at one month. — [López-de-Uralde-Villanueva et al. 2023 (abstract via QxMD)](https://read.qxmd.com/read/37582474/minimal-clinically-important-differences-in-hand-pain-intensity-numerical-pain-rate-scale-and-related-function-boston-carpal-tunnel-questionnaire-in-women-with-carpal-tunnel-syndrome)
- QuickDASH: 11 items scored 1–5, plus two optional 4-item modules (work; sport/performing arts) scored separately. — [IWH: About the QuickDASH](https://dash.iwh.on.ca/about-quickdash)
- QuickDASH licence: free without a licence for non-profit use and when not sold or built into a sold product; commercial use needs a fee-bearing limited licence; items, response options and scoring must not be changed; anyone embedding it in an EMR is told to contact IWH; official translations exist in 54 languages. — [IWH DASH FAQ](https://dash.iwh.on.ca/faq)
- QuickDASH change thresholds per the developer: MCID more than 8 points; MDC95 16–20 (mean 18) across three populations. For full DASH: MCID estimates 3.9–15, MDC95 mean 13. — [IWH DASH FAQ](https://dash.iwh.on.ca/faq)
- Slovenian QuickDASH: translated in 2014 and available from the DASH site; a Slovenian review of 16 studies reports Cronbach alpha about 0.83–0.94 and MCID estimates of 8–19 points, and notes that psychometrics of the Slovenian version itself were not published. — [Podlogar & Puh 2017, Fizioterapija 25(1):44–52](https://dlib.si/stream/URN:NBN:SI:DOC-JQUB0HRO/f1ecfd6a-d99e-476f-84a2-e2da08084b80/PDF)
- Expected post-CTR course that single items should track: night-time symptoms settle straight after surgery; scar soreness for a few weeks, settling by about three months; pain at the base of the palm on firm grip ("pillar"-type pain) for about 2–12 weeks; full grip power by 6–12 weeks; stitches out at 10–14 days. — [Royal College of Surgeons of England: Get Well Soon, carpal tunnel release](https://www.rcseng.ac.uk/-/media/Files/RCS/Patient-care/Recovering-from-surgery/GWScarpaltunnelrelease.pdf)

**CIPN**
- FACT/GOG-NTX-4: 4 items, past 7 days, 5-point scale; English version has no fee but a licence should be obtained by registering with FACIT; non-commercial fees are usually waived for investigator-initiated research, students and clinical use; translations need registration. — [FACIT: FACT/GOG-NTX-4](https://www.facit.org/measures/fact-gog-ntx-4)
- FACT/GOG-Ntx 11-item subscale: items 0–4, past week, higher = worse; Cronbach alpha 0.82–0.89; correlates r = 0.79–0.93 with QLQ-CIPN20; sensitivity to change small to moderate (r = 0.17–0.37); distribution-based MCID 1.38–3.68 points (earlier FACIT-based estimates 3.3–4.4); floor effects noted; n=343 (Hong Kong, Singapore, UK). — [Cheng et al. 2020, Health Qual Life Outcomes 18:246, doi:10.1186/s12955-020-01493-y](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12955-020-01493-y)
- PRO-CTCAE item library (version dated 12 May 2025) contains "Numbness & tingling" with two attributes, severity and interference; also fatigue (severity, interference), dizziness (severity, interference), general pain / muscle pain / joint pain (frequency, severity, interference). — [NCI PRO-CTCAE item library](https://healthcaredelivery.cancer.gov/pro-ctcae/item-library.pdf)
- PRO-CTCAE is available in more than 60 validated languages; terms of use sit on a separate page not read here. — [NCI PRO-CTCAE](https://healthcaredelivery.cancer.gov/pro-ctcae/)
- EORTC QLQ-CIPN20 is an EORTC module with phase III testing completed; access is by request form under EORTC user terms. — [EORTC Quality of Life Group: QLQ-CIPN20](https://qol.eortc.org/questionnaire/qlq-cipn20/)
- ESMO–EONS–EANO guideline on anticancer-therapy neurotoxicity (Jordan et al., Ann Oncol 2020) includes a practical algorithm for assessing chemotherapy-induced peripheral neurotoxicity. Only the summary page was readable. — [ESMO guideline page](https://esmo.org/guidelines/supportive-and-palliative-care/neurotoxicity)

### Inferences
- Daily-capable: self-authored single items (0–10 pain; 0–4 numbness/tingling severity and interference; yes/no events). Weekly-capable: PHQ-2 (2 items), a 4-item neuropathy block, 5 hand-function questions, QuickDASH (11) or BCTQ-style block (19 items, about 5–6 minutes). Baseline/monthly only: PROMIS-10, SIS-16.
- Because MDC is larger than MCID for SIS domains and QuickDASH, the doctor dashboard should trend weekly questionnaire scores over several weeks rather than alert on one change; the games supply the fine-grained signal.
- BCTQ symptom scores move fast and function scores move slowly after surgery, so the CTR daily check-in should weight symptoms (pain, numbness, night waking) early and function later.
- Safest licence posture for the demo: PHQ-2 and PROMIS-10 style content for stroke; QuickDASH verbatim only (Slovenian official text, unmodified, non-commercial) or else self-written items; self-written neuropathy items modelled on the severity/interference structure.

### Gaps
- Exact item wording and score ranges for FACT/GOG-NTX-4, QLQ-CIPN20 and BCTQ were not on the pages read. Unverified recall: NTX-4 covers numbness/tingling and discomfort in hands and in feet (total 0–16); CIPN20 has sensory, motor and autonomic subscales, 1–4 response scale, past week; BCTQ SSS includes night pain and night waking items.
- QuickDASH scoring formula and minimum answered items were not on the pages read (unverified recall: at least 10 of 11 items; score = (mean − 1) × 25, range 0–100).
- Slovenian versions of PROMIS-10, SIS/SIS-16, BCTQ, FACT/GOG-NTX, QLQ-CIPN20, PRO-CTCAE and PHQ-2: not confirmed. A McGill list from April 2020 showed no Slovenian PROMIS entries, but it lists only French, so it is not evidence either way — [McGill CAN-PRO](https://www.mcgill.ca/can-pro-network/node/630). Check the HealthMeasures language file, FACIT language list, EORTC translations page and MAPI ePROVIDE.
- Motor Activity Log short forms, self-reported mRS validity, TNSr (reduced Total Neuropathy Score) and stroke-specific PHQ-2 accuracy: no source read. TNSr is clinician-scored in part, so it is unlikely to suit self-report (unverified recall).
- PRO-CTCAE response wording, 7-day recall and permission terms not confirmed from the pages read.
- MCID for PROMIS-10 in stroke: not found.

## 2. Defensible minimal daily check-in (3–5 items, under a minute) and red-flag questions with guidance behind them

### Takeaway
A 4–5 item daily check-in is defensible if it is one red-flag gate plus two or three symptom ratings and one event question; the red-flag wording can be taken almost directly from NHS stroke advice, NHS hand-surgery aftercare leaflets and Macmillan neuropathy advice. The response to a "yes" must differ by severity: stroke signs go straight to an emergency-call screen, wound and neuropathy flags go to "contact your team/GP" plus a clinician alert.

### Cited Findings
- Stroke warning signs listed by the NHS: face drooping on one side, inability to raise and hold both arms, slurred or confused speech; also one-sided weakness or numbness, blurred or lost vision, trouble finding words, confusion, dizziness or falling, severe headache. Action: call emergency services at once, including when signs occurred in the last 24 hours and have stopped; do not drive yourself. — [NHS: Stroke symptoms](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/stroke/symptoms/)
- CTR wound and hand warning signs needing urgent GP review: hand swollen, hot, red or more painful; bad smell, blood-stained discharge or wound opening; fever with redness around the wound, or a rash; hand becoming numb; unable to make a full fist or fingers no less stiff at three weeks; swelling not improving by six weeks; scar still sensitive at six weeks. — [Oxford University Hospitals NHS: carpal tunnel leaflet](https://www.ouh.nhs.uk/media/aa2fevp5/122322tunnel.pdf)
- A second NHS trust gives the same core rule (red, hot, swollen or more painful than usual means urgent GP contact), adds that tingling which fails to settle or worsens should be reported, and states pain should not be severe. Dressings stay dry for two weeks. — [Royal Berkshire NHS: carpal tunnel decompression aftercare, July 2025](https://royalberkshire.nhs.uk/media/slrb4mev/carpal-tunnel-decompression-aftercare_jul25.pdf)
- Normal, non-alarming CTR findings to avoid false alerts: stiffness and weakness for around three months; scar sensitive to pressure for several weeks; about 3% recurrence. — [Royal Berkshire NHS aftercare](https://royalberkshire.nhs.uk/media/slrb4mev/carpal-tunnel-decompression-aftercare_jul25.pdf)
- A leaflet hosted by BSSH says scar tenderness and stiffness are common and usually settle, tingling often improves quickly, and grip strength takes longer. — [BSSH-hosted carpal tunnel leaflet](https://www.bssh.ac.uk/_userfiles/pages/files/Patients/Conditions/Carpal%20tunnel%20syndrome%20with%20logos.pdf)
- CIPN symptoms patients are told to report: tingling/numbness, trouble with buttons or picking up small objects, burning or very sensitive skin, pain, weakness making walking or stairs hard, balance or coordination problems, dizziness on standing, reduced hot/cold sensation, constipation. New or worsening symptoms should be reported as soon as noticed; the team checks before each treatment and may lower the dose or stop the drug. Symptoms can worsen for a few weeks after stopping. — [Macmillan Cancer Support: Peripheral neuropathy](https://www.macmillan.org.uk/cancer-information-and-support/impacts-of-cancer/peripheral-neuropathy)
- Falls were among the domains asked weekly in the PRO-TECT oncology symptom-monitoring trial. — [Oncology Nursing News on PRO-TECT](https://oncnursingnews.com/view/pro-tect-trial-shows-that-epros-may-be-feasibly-implemented-into-routine-oncology-practice)
- ICHOM single items for stroke function are ambulation, toileting, dressing, feeding and communication. — [ICHOM Stroke Reference Guide](https://ichom.org/files/medical-conditions/stroke/stroke-reference-guide.pdf)

### Inferences (proposed content; all wording self-authored, not from licensed instruments)
- **Stroke, daily (4 items):** (1) Red-flag gate, yes/no with pictograms: "Since yesterday, anything NEW and sudden: face drooping, arm or leg weak or numb, trouble speaking, loss of vision, very bad headache?" Yes leads to a full-screen emergency-call instruction (local emergency number) plus clinician alert. (2) "How well did your affected hand work today?" 5 faces. (3) Tiredness today, 0–10 or 5 faces. (4) "Did you fall since yesterday?" yes/no. **Weekly add-on:** PHQ-2 (2 items) and 5 self-written hand-use questions. **Baseline, day 7 and day 90:** PROMIS-10-type global health, matching ICHOM timing.
- **CTR, daily (5 items; wound item only until the wound has healed, roughly the first 2–3 weeks):** (1) Wound gate: "Is the hand red, hot, more swollen or more painful than yesterday? Any discharge, bad smell, or wound opening? Fever?" (2) Hand pain now, 0–10 (alert logic can use the 1.5-point MCID as a meaningful rise). (3) Numbness/tingling in the fingers today, 0–4. (4) "How many times did hand symptoms wake you last night?" 0, 1, 2, 3+. (5) After week 2: pain at the base of the palm when gripping, 0–4. **Weekly:** 8 self-written function questions or official QuickDASH unmodified.
- **CIPN, daily (4–5 items):** (1) Numbness or tingling in hands or feet, severity 0–4. (2) How much it interfered with daily activities, 0–4. (3) "Trouble with buttons or picking up small things today?" yes/no. (4) "Unsteady on your feet or a fall since yesterday?" yes/no (a fall alerts the team). (5) Optional pain 0–10. **Before each chemotherapy cycle:** an automatic summary for the oncology team, since guidance says the team reviews nerve symptoms before every treatment and may change the dose.
- Keep the red-flag gate as the first item every session so it is answered even if the patient abandons the rest.

### Gaps
- No Slovenian national guidance was read for any of the red flags; the sources are UK patient information. The Slovenian emergency number and local pathway wording must be confirmed by the team's clinical partner.
- ASCO CIPN guideline (Loprinzi et al., J Clin Oncol 2020) and the full ESMO text were not accessible, so dose-modification thresholds are not cited.
- AAOS aftercare material was not read.
- No evidence was found that a specific 3–5 item daily set has been validated for any of the three conditions.

## 3. Response burden and adherence: item count, frequency, time of day, reminders, feedback, drop-off

### Takeaway
Adherence falls over weeks even in supported trials (about 72% to 58% across 18 weeks of weekly reporting), and the interventions with measured effect are reminders plus a human follow-up of non-responders; questionnaire length alone is a weak predictor of burden, but daily diaries are explicitly flagged as burdensome. Patients over 70 complete less often, take longer and need help more often, so assisted first use and a proxy path matter more than shaving items.

### Cited Findings
- eRAPID RCT (n=508, chemotherapy, weekly online symptom reports for 18 weeks with weekly text/email reminders): mean adherence to weekly reporting about 64.6%; 71.8% in week 1 falling to 58.1% in week 18; reports per patient ranged 0–117 (median 14). — [Absolom et al. 2021, J Clin Oncol, eRAPID (author PDF)](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/169889/7/jco.20.02015.pdf)
- PRO-TECT cluster trial (52 US community oncology practices): weekly survey completion averaged 91.5%, with electronic reminders and a staff phone call within 72 hours to non-completers; patients could choose web or automated telephone, and many rural patients chose the phone. — [Oncology Nursing News on PRO-TECT](https://oncnursingnews.com/view/pro-tect-trial-shows-that-epros-may-be-feasibly-implemented-into-routine-oncology-practice)
- Danish myeloma cohort (271 patients, 1,441 scheduled assessments over 24 months): 84% completed on time, rising to 95% after an automatic e-mail reminder on day 4 and nurse contact on day 7; completion of outstanding forms rose from 16.1% to 25.5% on the reminder day and from 15.7% to 30.7% on the nurse-contact day; frailty, poor performance status and paper mode predicted non-completion. Observational pre/post, no control group. — [Nielsen et al. 2020, Qual Life Res, doi:10.1007/s11136-019-02304-8](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11136-019-02304-8)
- Daily ePRO in three respiratory trials (1,342 patients, handheld devices): adherence declined after randomisation, more steeply in younger patients; adherence in the first 14 days predicted adherence for the rest of the study; adherence was higher on working days than non-working days; the oldest patients took about twice as long per entry; response time fell during the first month. Industry-authored; abstract only. — [Nowojewski et al. 2024, Qual Life Res, doi:10.1007/s11136-024-03644-w](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11136-024-03644-w)
- 5,571 Austrian cancer rehabilitation patients completing a 30-item ePRO: completion above 90% up to age 70, 81.4% over 70 at home; 19% needed help at home versus 6.8% on site; need for help far higher over 80 (multivariable OR 17.56 vs age 50 or under); mean completion about 5 minutes, falling to 4 on the second occasion; cognitive functioning was not associated with completion. The summary of this paper noted an internal inconsistency in the direction of one odds ratio. — [Riedl et al. 2023, J Med Internet Res 25:e49476](https://www.jmir.org/2023/1/e49476)
- Respondent-burden review: length alone does not reliably predict burden (a full 80-item library was completed with high compliance), but daily diaries are burdensome and generate missing data; frequency should match the construct (symptoms more often than function or quality of life); allow ad hoc reports when the patient feels unwell; aim for about sixth-grade reading level; useful ePRO features are an estimated completion time, a progress indicator, graphs of one's own data, encouraging messages and a thank-you message; evidence on one-question-per-screen is mixed; explain why data matter and share results back, otherwise motivation drops. The review does not address reminders. — [Aiyegbusi et al. 2022, Nat Commun (author PDF)](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/files/178816039/AiyegbusiO2022Key.pdf)
- Older lung-cancer patients using an ePRO app (n=35, 57% aged 70+): adherence tended to drop when symptoms were stable; some patients were unsure their data had been sent and asked for a confirmation message; family members often entered data for the oldest patients. — [JMIR Cancer 2026;12:e97890](https://cancer.jmir.org/2026/1/e97890)
- Stroke: in a one-week Swiss telerehabilitation app test, 8 of 161 screened patients completed; training adherence was high in 1, moderate in 3, low in 1 and nil in 3, while passive activity-tracker wear was high in 6 of 8. — [Ziller et al. 2026, JMIR Rehabil Assist Technol, e77090](https://rehab.jmir.org/2026/1/e77090/PDF)
- Stroke: 15 working-age survivors in telerehabilitation, 60% adherent (5+ days/week over 4 weeks); barriers were post-stroke exhaustion, low digital confidence, unstable internet, doubt about benefit, "digital fatigue" and fear of re-injury; facilitators were seeing recovery, family support, therapist supervision and fitting it into a routine. — [Huang et al. 2026, Front Neurol](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2026.1885751/full)
- Aphasia app RCT: participants logged 19.5 hours (SD 8.6) over 5 weeks against a prescribed 25, despite weekly staff check-ins. — [Kim et al. 2025, JMIR mHealth uHealth 13:e67711](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2025/1/e67711)

### Inferences
- Plan for roughly a 15–25% relative decline in completion over the first months even with reminders; the trend algorithm must tolerate gaps rather than assume daily data.
- The first two weeks are the leverage point (early adherence predicts later adherence): put the most onboarding support, the baseline sessions and a clinician "welcome" message there.
- Build a two-step escalation: automatic reminder on the same day, then a flag on the doctor dashboard after 2–3 missed days so a human can call. This mirrors the only designs with measured gains.
- Expect weekend dips; a gentler weekend mode (questions only, games optional) is reasonable.
- "No change" days are when patients stop reporting, so the stable state needs its own reward (confirmation, streak, a short message that stable is good news).
- Always show a "sent to your care team" confirmation after submission.

### Gaps
- No study read tested time of day for prompts, or push versus SMS head to head. No randomised test of reminders alone was read.
- No evidence found on an optimal item count for a daily check-in, or on daily versus weekly schedules compared directly in older adults or stroke survivors.
- Week-by-week drop-off curves for daily self-reporting in stroke survivors: not found. The systematic review on perseverance with home upper-limb technology after stroke (PMC7905577) was blocked.

## 4. Accessibility and usability for older adults, hemiparesis, tremor, numb fingers, aphasia, cognitive impairment, low vision; caregiver/proxy use

### Takeaway
WCAG 2.2 gives the checkable minimums (24 px targets, 4.5:1 contrast, 200% resize, no drag-only or motion-only controls, adjustable time limits, no memory-test logins), but studies of older users and stroke survivors argue for going well beyond them: much larger well-spaced buttons, large sans-serif text, minimal text with bold key words and pictures, spoken output, forgiving input, stable layouts and a sanctioned helper role. The motor games conflict with WCAG by design (dragging, motion, timing), so they need to be framed as measurements with an accessible way to skip or get help.

### Cited Findings
- WCAG 2.2 new criteria relevant here: 2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum), AA: targets at least 24 by 24 CSS px or equivalently spaced; 2.5.7 Dragging Movements, AA: any drag function also works by single tap/click unless dragging is essential; 3.3.7 Redundant Entry, A; 3.3.8 Accessible Authentication (Minimum), AA: no cognitive function test such as remembering a password unless an alternative or assistance mechanism exists; 3.2.6 Consistent Help, A; 2.4.11 Focus Not Obscured, AA. — [W3C WAI: What's New in WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/new-in-22/)
- Other WCAG 2.2 criteria: 1.4.3 contrast at least 4.5:1 (3:1 for large text), AA; 1.4.6 enhanced 7:1, AAA; 1.4.4 text resizable to 200%, AA; 1.4.10 reflow at 320 CSS px width, AA; 1.4.11 non-text contrast 3:1, AA; 1.4.1 colour not the only cue, A; 2.2.1 time limits can be turned off, adjusted or extended, A; 2.2.3 no timing, AAA; 2.3.3 interaction-triggered animation can be disabled, AAA; 2.5.1 path-based or multipoint gestures have a single-pointer alternative unless essential, A; 2.5.2 pointer cancellation (no action on down-event), A; 2.5.4 motion-operated functions also operable by on-screen controls and motion response can be disabled, A. — [W3C: WCAG 2.2 Recommendation](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)
- Users 65+ versus 21–55 on websites: task success 55.3% vs 74.5%; 43% slower; 2.4 vs 1.1 errors per session; about twice as likely to give up; 45% uncomfortable trying new things; seniors blamed themselves for problems 90% of the time. Recommendations: at least 12-point default text with user enlargement, large well-separated targets, no pull-down or cascading menus, clear error messages with easy fixes, tolerant input formats, and no drastic redesigns because about half of older users keep written notes of steps. Study from 2013. — [Nielsen Norman Group: Usability for Seniors](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-seniors-improvements/)
- Aphasia-accessible written information: simple language, large sans-serif font, key words in bold, little text with generous spacing, and images unless the person finds them unhelpful (evidence on pictures is mixed across studies); there is no agreed definition of "aphasia friendly"; adapted material gets longer, which some readers dislike. — [Grobler, Casey & Farrell 2022, ACNR, doi:10.47795/wkdo3084](https://acnr.co.uk/articles/making-information-accessible-for-people-with-aphasia/)
- Stroke Association practice: clear typeface of at least 12 point, good text/background contrast, white space around text and pictures, plus large-print, electronic (screen-reader) and audio formats. — [Stroke Association: Accessible information](https://www.stroke.org.uk/our-publications/accessible-information)
- App for expressive aphasia (qualitative, small sample; SUS mean 75 in 4 users): users praised simplicity, font size, colour, alignment and spacing; requested adjustable font size, bold key words, changeable background/text colours, spoken output, graphics in place of text and a choice between two options; one-sided weakness may need alternatives to tapping; cognitive problems make self-checking burdensome; first-use training by a therapist or family member or an in-app video was recommended. — [R-SPEAK study, J Med Internet Res 2026;28:e93518](https://www.jmir.org/2026/1/e93518)
- Home upper-limb system for chronic stroke (n=11, 6 weeks): 5 participants did not understand the numeric scores (three decimals were meaningless); users forgot what the success icon and sound meant and rarely opened the manual; larger font was valued; verbal feedback and a best-score display were requested; a repetition counter that lagged caused confusion; 4 had a caregiver help (3 for app navigation, all 4 for physical set-up); sessions needing 40–45 minutes were hard to fit around caregiver availability. — [Bhattacharjya et al. 2021, JMIR Hum Factors 8(1):e21312](https://humanfactors.jmir.org/2021/1/e21312)
- Speech recognition was unreliable for people with motor speech impairment and caused one withdrawal; app SUS averaged 61.2. — [Kim et al. 2025, JMIR mHealth uHealth 13:e67711](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2025/1/e67711)
- Stroke telerehabilitation app: several patients needed substantial help with chat and most needed help with a blood-pressure device; menu navigation caused minor trouble; technical faults blocked exercise on some days. — [Ziller et al. 2026, e77090](https://rehab.jmir.org/2026/1/e77090/PDF)
- Proxy reporting: ICHOM allows a proxy, clinician or records to answer when the patient cannot; SIS proxies rate patients as more impaired; family-entered ePRO may overstate symptom severity; regulators discourage proxy reports so clear rules are needed; pictures, icons or emoticon response versions are recommended for people who struggle with standard PROMs. — [ICHOM Stroke Reference Guide](https://ichom.org/files/medical-conditions/stroke/stroke-reference-guide.pdf); [SRAlab SIS](https://www.sralab.org/rehabilitation-measures/stroke-impact-scale); [JMIR Cancer 2026 e97890](https://cancer.jmir.org/2026/1/e97890); [Aiyegbusi et al. 2022](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/files/178816039/AiyegbusiO2022Key.pdf)

### Inferences (build rules)
- One question per screen, at most 2–5 very large answer buttons stacked vertically and reachable with one thumb; no sliders that require dragging (offer tap-on-number 0–10), no dropdowns, no hover-only or double-tap actions; act on release, with an "Undo/Back" always in the same place.
- Base text about 18–20 px with a persistent "A+" control; contrast aimed at 7:1; never colour alone (pair with icon and word).
- Each question: one short sentence, one bold key word, one pictogram, and a "read aloud" button (browser speech synthesis is a low-cost way to do this).
- No countdowns in the questionnaire. In games, timing is the measurement, so state plainly that it is a test, allow unlimited practice and a "not today / I need help" exit that is logged as a reason rather than as missing data.
- Login without passwords (magic link, QR code or device-remembered session) to satisfy 3.3.8 and reduce drop-out at the door.
- Record who answered (patient alone, patient with help, helper for patient) on every session; proxies skew scores, so the dashboard should display the flag.
- Let the patient choose the hand used and say so in instructions; numb fingers and tremor argue for generous spacing between targets and ignoring repeated taps within a short interval.
- Keep layouts unchanged between sessions; older users rely on memorised or written steps.
- Show results as words and simple pictures, not decimals.

### Gaps
- WCAG 2.5.5 (enhanced target size), 3.1.5 (reading level) and 3.3.1–3.3.4 (error handling) were beyond the portion of the specification retrieved (unverified recall: 2.5.5 is AAA at 44 by 44 CSS px).
- Rose et al. (2011, 2012) primary aphasia-friendly studies and the Stroke Association's detailed guideline document were not accessible; no numeric rules for sentence length were verified.
- No controlled evidence found on optimal touch-target size for hemiparesis, tremor or neuropathic fingers specifically; a systematic review on font size for older mobile users (PMC9376262) was blocked.
- NN/g's newer report on older adults was not read; the cited figures are from 2013.

## 5. What patients should see about their own results; safe messaging on deterioration

### Takeaway
Feedback to patients is recommended as a motivation device and its absence is linked to falling completion, with consensus guidance favouring simple line graphs of one's own scores over time, consistent direction, sparing use of labels and a clear but calm marker for possibly concerning results. Evidence that showing declining scores causes anxiety was not found either way; the working model from oncology is tiered automated advice (self-care, contact the team, clinician alert), in which true emergency alerts were under 1% of reports.

### Cited Findings
- Consensus recommendations for showing PRO data to patients: line graphs are preferred for an individual's scores over time; do not mix score directions in one display and label direction very clearly; descriptive y-axis labels (none/mild/moderate/severe) help where evidence supports their placement; results that are possibly concerning should be shown, consistently with how other clinical data are flagged; patients tend to value an indication of concerning worsening; displays must still work without colour; if a comparison group is shown, call it "average", describe it, and say it may not apply to the individual. — [Snyder et al. 2019, Qual Life Res, "Making a picture worth a thousand numbers" (author manuscript)](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/138777/3/Snyder%20PRO%20display%20manuscript-QLR-revised-v2-SUBMIT.pdf)
- Tiered automated advice in eRAPID: of 3,314 online reports, 81.9% produced self-management advice, 13.9% (serious symptoms or three or more moderate ones) produced advice to contact the hospital, and 0.9% triggered an emergency alert to nurses. Patients in the intervention arm had better physical well-being at 6 and 12 weeks and higher self-efficacy at 18 weeks, with no increase in admissions or hotline calls. — [Absolom et al. 2021, eRAPID](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/169889/7/jco.20.02015.pdf)
- PRO-TECT: nurses received alerts for worsening or new symptoms; 77% of patients felt more in control of their care, 73% said it improved discussions, 89% would recommend it; about a quarter of nurses worried about workload. — [Oncology Nursing News on PRO-TECT](https://oncnursingnews.com/view/pro-tect-trial-shows-that-epros-may-be-feasibly-implemented-into-routine-oncology-practice)
- Qualitative study of PROM feedback reports (25 hip/knee patients aged 49–82, all with adequate health literacy): scores taught patients something new, confirmed what they knew or gave a frame of reference; reports were described as reassuring and reduced fear; some saw a score as a snapshot that missed fluctuating symptoms; authors recommend more frequent monitoring outside visits and implicit comparison through colour coding. — [Stern et al. 2022, BMC Musculoskelet Disord, doi:10.1186/s12891-022-05764-1](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1186/s12891-022-05764-1.pdf)
- Sharing results back and showing how they inform care sustains motivation; graphs of personal data, encouraging and thank-you messages are listed as helpful ePRO features. — [Aiyegbusi et al. 2022](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/files/178816039/AiyegbusiO2022Key.pdf)
- Stroke users worked harder to earn a positive icon and sound (9 of 11 liked it), asked for a best-score display, and could not interpret raw numbers. — [Bhattacharjya et al. 2021](https://humanfactors.jmir.org/2021/1/e21312)
- Seeing functional recovery was itself an adherence facilitator for stroke survivors; doubt about benefit was a barrier; progress visualisation was recommended. — [Huang et al. 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2026.1885751/full)
- Older ePRO users wanted a confirmation that data reached the team and a built-in reference guide on side effects. — [JMIR Cancer 2026 e97890](https://cancer.jmir.org/2026/1/e97890)
- Emergency wording must not be softened for stroke signs: guidance is to call emergency services immediately even if signs have stopped. — [NHS: Stroke symptoms](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/stroke/symptoms/)

### Inferences (three-tier message scheme)
- **Tier 0, normal or improved:** "Thank you. Your results were sent to your care team." Show own trend line with "you, over the last 2 weeks" and personal bests. No population norms, no diagnostic words.
- **Tier 1, algorithm-detected gradual decline in game metrics or scores:** patient sees neutral wording such as "Your results this week were a little different from last week. Your care team can see this and will contact you if needed. Results vary from day to day." The clinician gets the alert. Avoid red colour and words like "deterioration" or "worse" on the patient side, because the algorithm is unvalidated and day-to-day variation is large relative to MCID.
- **Tier 2, patient-reported red flag (wound signs, fall, new numbness, mood screen positive):** "Please contact your clinic or GP today" with a tap-to-call number, plus a clinician alert.
- **Tier 3, stroke signs:** full-screen "Call the emergency number now" and nothing else; never only "we have notified your team".
- State service limits on every result screen: the app is not watched around the clock and does not replace emergency care.
- Present game results as "steadiness", "speed" or "reach" in words or simple bars, with direction always "up is better".

### Gaps
- No study was found that directly measures anxiety caused by showing patients a declining trend, nor a trial comparing "we've notified your team" with "see a doctor now" wording. Papers on patient experience of PROM feedback (PMC9981839, PMC8272760, PMC11599350) were blocked.
- No guidance found on patient-facing display of unvalidated sensor-derived motor metrics; the tiering above is a design judgement and needs clinical sign-off.
- Regulatory status (medical device software rules for automated advice) was out of scope and not researched.

## 6. Gamification elements that sustain adherence in older/clinical populations, and what backfires

### Takeaway
Evidence for gamification in older adults is positive but thin: a small pooled enjoyment effect (SMD 0.34, low certainty), no study isolating single elements, and the longest intervention 14 weeks. The best-supported ingredients are user control over difficulty and rewards, personalised feedback, visible progress, involvement of a support partner or clinician, and tasks that feel relevant to daily life; the documented ways it fails are usability faults, wrong difficulty, repetitive content and incomprehensible scores rather than any named game mechanic.

### Cited Findings
- Scoping review of mHealth gamification in older adults (11 studies, 545 participants, search to Dec 2025): most-used elements were progress, challenges, goals, levels and rewards; no study used social sharing; no element was identified as harmful; negative experiences were system freezes, hard mechanics, small on-screen objects, mismatch between real and in-game movement, stress and fatigue; only 4 studies used a theory; longest intervention 14 weeks. — [Chen et al. 2026, JMIR mHealth uHealth 14:e82368](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2026/1/e82368)
- Within that review: giving older users control over difficulty, reward choice and visual theme raised play frequency and total engagement time (n=21, non-randomised, elements bundled); gamification plus a support partner added 1,699 steps/day versus control in 94 at-risk older adults, partly sustained three weeks later; personalised feedback with collaborative goal setting raised engagement in pulmonary telerehabilitation (uncontrolled). — [Chen et al. 2026](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2026/1/e82368)
- Meta-analysis of active video games in older adults (5 studies, 419 participants, mean age 74.7): enjoyment/satisfaction SMD 0.34 (95% CI 0.05–0.64), low certainty and sensitive to one study; the only study measuring adherence found 85% overall but more dropouts from dissatisfaction in the game arm than in control; barriers included loss of interest, technical difficulty and embarrassment. — [Bravo-Aparicio et al. 2025, JMIR Aging 8:e72559](https://aging.jmir.org/2025/1/e72559/PDF)
- Chronic stroke home system: an immediate positive icon plus sound motivated extra repetitions; participants wanted tasks that were neither too hard (some abandoned one task) nor too easy (seen as useless), preferred tasks resembling real life, and asked for best-score tracking. — [Bhattacharjya et al. 2021](https://humanfactors.jmir.org/2021/1/e21312)
- Stroke telerehabilitation: repetitive app content came to feel like a chore; recommendations were progress visualisation, varied content, serious games, family as co-facilitators, goals tied to the person's aims (for this sample, return to work) and flexible scheduling. — [Huang et al. 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2026.1885751/full)
- Stroke telerehabilitation app: some exercises were too easy; authors recommend difficulty progression and remote therapist support, which they link to adherence. — [Ziller et al. 2026](https://rehab.jmir.org/2026/1/e77090/PDF)
- Older users dislike wasted time and tend to blame themselves for interface failures. — [Nielsen Norman Group](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-seniors-improvements/)
- ePRO authors propose gamification or behavioural-economics features to sustain long-term reporting in older cancer patients (proposal, untested). — [JMIR Cancer 2026 e97890](https://cancer.jmir.org/2026/1/e97890)

### Inferences
- Lowest-risk set for 20 hours: (a) immediate per-game success feedback (icon plus sound, with the meaning restated each time); (b) a personal-best and "your last 7 days" strip; (c) a forgiving streak counted per week (for example 5 of 7 days) so one missed day does not reset progress; (d) a short visible message that the clinician has looked at the data; (e) a difficulty choice for a practice round kept separate from the fixed-difficulty measurement round, since changing difficulty would corrupt the trend signal.
- Avoid leaderboards and comparison with other patients: no supporting evidence was found, progression differs by condition, and self-blame is common in this age group. Avoid childish themes, loss-framed streak resets, point totals without meaning, and long animations.
- Rotate cosmetic variety (themes, backgrounds) while keeping the measured task identical, to counter repetition without breaking comparability.
- Tie the "why" to a personal goal captured at onboarding (buttons, writing, cooking, returning to work) and reference it in feedback.

### Gaps
- No trial isolating streaks, badges or leaderboards in older or neurological patients was found; claims that specific mechanics backfire are not evidence-based in the sources read.
- "The Potential and Pitfalls of Gamification to Support Older Adults' Adherence" (PMC6840662) and the Tampere gamification review were blocked.
- Long-term (beyond 14 weeks) adherence effects of gamification: no data found.

## 7. Onboarding: consent, explaining purpose, baseline sessions, device checks, trust and privacy

### Takeaway
Older adults are more reluctant to adopt remote-care technology, and identifiable video is the least accepted data type, while most people are willing to share with their healthcare providers when the benefit is explained; so onboarding should lead with who sees the data and why, and say explicitly what the camera does and does not keep. Assisted first use is the norm in studies that worked (in-clinic training, family help, a help line), and the first two weeks set the adherence pattern.

### Cited Findings
- Canadian survey (384 respondents aged 20–93, median 53) on remote healthcare technology: comfort was highest for wellness/activity data (mean 3.8 of 5) and lowest for identifiable video (3.2); identifiable video raised concern in 32%; top concerns were misuse (34%) and storage/protection (29%); 83% would share with healthcare providers, 62% with immediate family, 5% with no one; adults 65+ reported lower intention to use (3.3) than ages 35–49 (4.1) or 50–64 (3.9); the authors advise explaining how sharing benefits care, considering anonymous alternatives to identifiable video, and addressing misuse and storage directly. Preprint, self-selected sample. — [Napoli et al. 2025, arXiv:2501.14098](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.14098)
- At-home first completion needed help in 19% of patients versus 6.8% on site; authors suggest relatives, a help hotline and building eHealth self-efficacy for older patients. — [Riedl et al. 2023](https://www.jmir.org/2023/1/e49476)
- Stroke users trained 40–60 minutes in the lab before six weeks of home use and still forgot what feedback symbols meant and rarely used the printed manual. — [Bhattacharjya et al. 2021](https://humanfactors.jmir.org/2021/1/e21312)
- Recommended pre-enrolment screening for stroke telerehabilitation: digital literacy, home environment and connectivity; family as co-facilitators; offline tolerance. — [Huang et al. 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2026.1885751/full)
- First-use training by a clinician or family member, or an in-app tutorial video, was recommended by people with aphasia and clinicians. — [R-SPEAK study 2026](https://www.jmir.org/2026/1/e93518)
- Patient-rated clarity of staff instructions for an ePRO app rose from a median of 5 to 8 out of 10 between treatment cycles 1 and 3; ePRO should sit inside a wider support programme, not stand alone. — [JMIR Cancer 2026 e97890](https://cancer.jmir.org/2026/1/e97890)
- Explaining the purpose of PRO collection and the effect of missing data helps patients see why completion matters. — [Aiyegbusi et al. 2022](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/files/178816039/AiyegbusiO2022Key.pdf)
- Adherence in the first 14 days predicted later adherence; response time shortened over the first month. — [Nowojewski et al. 2024](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11136-024-03644-w)
- Login must not depend on memorising a password unless an alternative exists (WCAG 3.3.8, AA); motion-operated functions need a way to disable or bypass (2.5.4, A). — [W3C WAI: New in WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/new-in-22/); [W3C WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)

### Inferences (onboarding script, about 5 screens)
- Screen 1, purpose in three pictogram lines: what you do each day (about 3 minutes), who sees it (your doctor), and why daily matters (gaps hide changes).
- Screen 2, limits: not monitored around the clock; in an emergency call the emergency number.
- Screen 3, privacy in plain words: "The camera is used only to measure hand movement. Video is not saved or sent; only numbers are." Make this true technically (process frames in the browser, upload landmarks/metrics only) because identifiable video is the least accepted data type. Offer a visible camera-off path that still lets the patient do the questionnaire and the mouse or phone game.
- Screen 4, device check as a guided "test run": camera permission, lighting/hand-in-frame check, phone motion-sensor permission (must be triggered by a tap), sound check for read-aloud. Each failure gets a fallback, never a dead end.
- Screen 5, helper and goals: who will help (nobody / family member), consent to helper entry, pick the affected or operated hand, pick one personal goal, choose reminder time and channel.
- Treat the first 2–3 sessions as "practice and baseline": no scores shown as good or bad, repeat instructions each time, and tell the patient that early learning improvements are expected.
- Ideally the first session happens before discharge with staff present; for the demo, show a "clinic-assisted setup" mode on the doctor side that issues a QR code.

### Gaps
- Two reviews on acceptance and privacy of camera-based monitoring in older adults (PMC10186188, PMC11833315) and a JMIR 2023 paper (e45297) could not be read; the privacy evidence above rests on a single preprint survey.
- GDPR consent wording for health data, Slovenian legal requirements and ethics approval needs were not researched.
- No study found on the number of baseline sessions needed before browser-based motor game scores stabilise (learning effect); this needs the team's own data or the companion research on the games.
- No evidence found on how patients react to browser permission prompts for camera and motion sensors specifically.
